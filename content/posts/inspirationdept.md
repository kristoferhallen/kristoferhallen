There's no shortage of good writing about how to build software, and it's great to learn, to not get stuck in your current world view. Newsletters, conference talks, blog posts. I read a lot. I get inspired.

And then nothing happens.

The ideas sit there. I have thoughts, mental models, half-formed plans. But they don't make it into practice. I move on to the next article, the next newsletter, the next conference recording. And the cycle repeats.
Let's call this Inspiration Debt. Every idea you consume but never apply adds to a growing backlog of "things I should try." And just like technical debt, at some point the weight of it becomes counterproductive. You spend more time thinking about what you could do than actually doing anything.

The dangerous part is that it feels productive. It feels like you're getting better. But if none of it changes what you do on Monday morning, you're just collecting inspiration for its own sake.
So how do you pay down inspiration debt? By turning ideas into experiments.

I don't think there's a universal answer, but here's what I've started doing to turn inspiration into something useful.
Start from your actual problems. Not from what's interesting to read about — from what you actually need to solve. What are the tasks in front of you? That context is your filter. It tells you which ideas are relevant right now and which ones are just interesting noise.

Pick one or two inspirations, not twenty. The goal is to understand an approach well enough to actually try it, not to survey the entire landscape of opinions.

Formulate a hypothesis. Take the idea and make it concrete: "If we adopt this approach, we expect to see this outcome within this timeframe." Write it down. Make it specific enough that you can verify or falsify it. Then test it. You're not just saying "that's a great idea", you're saying "I believe this will work in our context, and here's how we'll know."

None of this means you should stop reading broadly. Broad reading builds intuition, it gives you new perspectives and a base of knowledge to draw on when you encounter something unexpected. The problem isn't learning. The problem is when learning becomes the whole activity.
The next time you finish a great article and feel that spark of inspiration, ask yourself: what problem does this solve for me, specifically? If you have an answer, formulate your hypothesis and try it. If you don't, save it and get back to work.
