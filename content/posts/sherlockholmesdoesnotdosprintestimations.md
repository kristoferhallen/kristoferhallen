I have previously written about [Sherlock Holmes and software development]({{< relref "softwaredevelopmentaccordingtosherlock.md" >}}). What would be his opinion about planning estimations? He never promises Watson a timeline. He commits to a method: observe, reason, follow the evidence. "I'll have the murderer by Thursday" is not something Holmes says.

But Holmes does work against deadlines. In "The Adventure of the Norwood Builder", McFarlane is about to be convicted for a murder he didn't commit. Holmes doesn't estimate how long the case will take — he needs to be faster than the gaol. That distinction is everything. A deadline is external and real: the wedding is in three days, the client is on trial Friday. An estimation is a prediction about how long new work takes — and new work is always uncertain.

Holmes understands this. He never confuses the two. When pressed, he doesn't say "I'll have it solved in two days." He says: here's what I know, here's what I'm going to find out, and here's what I'll do next. That's not evasion. That's just honest.

Software teams often get this backwards. The sprint estimation meeting starts with "how many points is this?" before anyone understands what the work actually is. Then the estimate becomes a commitment, the commitment becomes a deadline, and when it slips, someone asks why we couldn't estimate better. Holmes would not recognize this as reasoning.

The honest answer to "how long will this take?" is almost always "it depends on what we find." It's an accurate description of new work. Estimating software is estimating investigation.

So when you have a real deadline — a release date, a compliance requirement, a demo that can't move — don't estimate. Instead: here's what we know, here's what we don't, and here's our best read on what fits in the window. Then revisit as you learn more.

Holmes never promises a timeline. He promises a method. That's the only commitment you can honestly make.
