The Consulting Detective´s Guide to Software Development: Lessons from Sherlock Holmes

15 Mar 2025

As an avid Sherlock Holmes reader and software engineer, I asked myself if the methods of the greatest detective are useful in a software development context. This isn’t a handbook on software development, but wouldn’t your day be a little more exciting if you approached it like Sherlock Holmes? Here are key lessons developers can learn from the master of deduction:

Partner for Perspective

"I am lost without my Boswell." — A Scandal in Bohemia

Holmes relied on Watson not just for companionship, but for his different perspective and complementary skills. In software development, collaboration brings diverse viewpoints that catch blind spots and spark innovation. No developer possesses every skill or perspective needed to build exceptional software. Find your Watson—someone whose strengths balance your weaknesses—and you’ll solve problems more effectively together.

Build Your Network

"These youngsters, however, go everywhere and hear everything. They are as sharp as needles, too; all they want is organization." — A Study in Scarlet

Holmes’ Baker Street Irregulars extended his reach throughout London. Similarly, cultivate your own network of specialists and collaborators. In today’s complex development environment, knowing who to call upon for specific expertise is often more valuable than trying to master everything yourself. Create your team of “irregulars”—mentors, peers, and specialists—who can help when challenges exceed your individual capabilities.

Deliberate Before Deciding

"It is quite a three pipe problem, and I beg that you won't speak to me for fifty minutes." — The Red-Headed League

Holmes understood the importance of deep thinking before action. When debugging or designing systems, resist the urge to immediately implement the first solution that comes to mind. Give yourself dedicated time for reflection—your own “three pipe” moments. Complex problems require methodical consideration of evidence and alternatives before determining the optimal approach.

Evidence-Based Development

"Data! Data! Data! I can't make bricks without clay." — The Adventure of the Copper Beeches Holmes refused to theorize before having sufficient facts. In software development, this translates to making decisions based on data rather than assumptions. Implement logging, metrics, and analytics to understand how your systems actually behave. Without meaningful data, you're building on speculation rather than reality.

Become a Knowledge Multiplier

"I'm a consulting detective... When these fellows are at fault they come to me, and I manage to put them on the right scent." — The Sign of Four

As you gain expertise, transition from individual contributor to knowledge sharer. Like Holmes assisting Scotland Yard, your greatest impact often comes from guiding others toward solutions rather than solving every problem yourself. Mentoring junior developers, conducting code reviews, and contributing to documentation multiplies your effectiveness across the entire organization.

Stay Close to the Code

"You know my methods, Watson. There was not one of them which I did not apply to the inquiry. And it ended by my discovering traces, but very different ones from those which I had expected." — The Crooked Man

Holmes never delegated the essential investigative work, even as his reputation grew. Similarly, as you advance in your career, don’t become detached from practical implementation. Regular hands-on coding keeps your skills sharp and your advice relevant. The most effective technical leaders remain practitioners who can still “get their hands dirty” with the details that matter.

Systematic Debugging

"When you have eliminated the impossible, whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth." — The Sign of Four

Holmes’ methodical elimination process mirrors effective debugging. Rather than jumping to conclusions about what’s causing a bug, systematically rule out possible causes through targeted testing and logging. Create hypotheses, test them rigorously, and be willing to consider explanations that initially seem unlikely. The most elusive bugs often have surprising causes that become obvious only after eliminating the expected culprits.

Design with Intent

"I never guess. It is a shocking habit—destructive to the logical faculty." — The Sign of Four

Holmes disdained guesswork, preferring methodical reasoning. In software architecture, this translates to intentional design rather than accidental complexity. Document your design decisions and their rationales. Understand why your system works the way it does, rather than allowing its architecture to emerge through a series of unplanned changes. Every significant aspect of your system should exist for a reason you can articulate.

Embrace Lifelong Learning

"Education never ends, Watson. It is a series of lessons, with the greatest for the last." — His Last Bow

Holmes constantly updated his knowledge, from chemistry to obscure literature. In our rapidly evolving field, continuous learning isn’t optional—it’s essential. Dedicate time to explore new technologies, methodologies, and domains. The most valuable developers aren’t those who know everything, but those who maintain a curiosity and adaptability throughout their careers.

“The game is afoot.” — The Adventure of the Abbey Grange

As Holmes himself maybe would have said, the difference between a good developer and a great one lies not in the tools they use, but in how they approach the mysteries they seek to solve.
